Java gives you automatic memory management, and then spends the rest of your
career making you care about it anyway.

The collector is not magic and it is not free. It is a program running inside
your program, and the only reason you notice it is that occasionally

<Underline>it stops yours</Underline>.

## Two places things live

A local variable holding a number lives on the **stack**, in the frame for the
method that declared it. When the method returns, the frame goes, and so does
the variable. No collector involved.

Anything you `new` lives on the **heap**, and the variable on the stack is a
reference to it. The heap is shared across threads and it is what the collector
manages.

So `int x = 5` and `Integer x = 5` are not the same shape at all, and neither is
`int[]` and `Integer[]`. One of those distinctions will cost you an interview
question and the other will cost you memory.

## The observation the whole design rests on

Most objects die young.

That is not a design decision, it is a measurement, and it holds across almost
every program anyone has looked at. A request comes in, allocates a pile of
short-lived objects, and finishes. Almost none of them survive.

So the heap is split. New objects go in a small **young generation**, and
collecting it means finding the few survivors and copying them out rather than
finding the many dead ones and cleaning them up. That is cheap, and it is cheap
precisely because most of what it looks at is garbage.

Survive enough of those collections and you get promoted to the **old
generation**, which is collected far less often and much more expensively.

The consequence worth carrying: <Highlight>allocation is cheap, promotion is
not</Highlight>. An object that lives slightly too long costs far more than one
that dies immediately.

## Why the pause happened

Some phases of collection need the heap to hold still. The collector cannot
chase references while your threads are busy rewriting them, so it stops them.
That is a **stop-the-world** pause, and it is the thing your users actually feel.

Modern collectors work hard to shrink it. G1, the default since Java 9, splits
the heap into regions and collects the ones with the most garbage first, which
is where the name comes from. ZGC and Shenandoah go further and do most of their
work concurrently, trading throughput for pauses that stay small even on large
heaps.

None of that makes pauses go away. It makes them shorter and more predictable,
which is usually what you actually wanted.

## What causes trouble in practice

**Holding references you have finished with.** A static `Map` used as a cache
with no eviction is the classic. Nothing is unreachable, so nothing is
collected, and the heap grows until it does not. This is the Java version of a
memory leak and it is entirely your doing.

**Allocating in a loop that did not need to.** String concatenation inside a
loop creates a new object each time round. `StringBuilder` exists for exactly
this and the compiler will not always rescue you.

**Assuming a bigger heap is a fix.** A larger heap often means longer
collections, not fewer problems. If you are promoting garbage, more room to
promote it into is not help.

## What to do when it happens

Not guess. There is real instrumentation here and almost nobody reaches for it.

Turn on GC logging and read it: how often collections run, how long they take,
and how much survives each one. If survivors are climbing steadily, something is
being retained. Take a heap dump and look at what holds the most, then follow
the reference chain back to whatever will not let go.

The answer is nearly always a collection someone forgot to bound.

## What an interviewer is checking

They are rarely testing whether you can name the generations. They are testing
whether you know that <Circle>you can still leak</Circle> in a garbage-collected
language, and whether you understand why.

If you can say "a leak in Java is an unwanted reference, not an unfreed
allocation", you have answered the real question.
