A viva is not a test of your code. It is a test of whether the code is yours.

The examiner has already seen it work. What they are deciding is whether you
made the decisions in it, and they find out by asking why, twice.

## The question underneath every question

Whatever they ask, they are asking one thing: <Underline>can you defend a
choice</Underline>.

"Why a `HashMap` here?" is not about `HashMap`. It is whether you considered
anything else. "Why is this an interface?" is not about interfaces. The good
answer always has the same shape: what the alternative was, and what made you
pick this one.

"Because that is what the tutorial used" is an honest answer and it is also the
one that costs you marks. "I used a `HashMap` because I need lookup by id and I
do not care about order; a `TreeMap` would have cost me log n for sorting I do
not use" is the same decision, defended.

## Where they will actually push

**The data model.** Why this table, why this key, why not one big table. If you
normalised, why. If you did not, why not.

**The one weird bit.** Every project has a part where something was worked
around. They will find it, and they are not looking for it to be elegant. They
are looking for you to know it is there and be able to say what you would do
with more time.

**Anything you cannot explain.** Which, increasingly, is the code an agent wrote
while you watched. If your commit history is clean and your explanation is not,
that gap is the whole viva.

## The three-sentence habit

Rehearse a three-sentence answer for every significant decision in the project.
What it does, why this way, what you gave up.

Three sentences, because the failure is almost never saying too little. It is
starting to explain, realising halfway that you are not sure, and trailing off.
An examiner reads that as not knowing, whether or not you did.

## What to do when you do not know

Say so, then say how you would find out.

"I am not sure, I would check what the query planner does with it" is a
genuinely good answer. It is honest, it shows method, and it is far stronger than
a guess delivered confidently. Examiners have heard a great many confident
guesses and they can tell.

The one thing that does real damage is <Highlight>defending something you do not
understand</Highlight>. It invites a follow-up you cannot survive, and now the
question is not about the code any more.

## Before the day

Read your own code as if someone else wrote it. Genuinely read it, and stop at
anything that makes you pause. Every one of those pauses is a question waiting to
be asked.

Run it once from a clean checkout. Projects that only work on the author's laptop
are found in about ninety seconds, and it is a bad ninety seconds.

Then pick the three decisions you are proudest of and make sure you can say each
in three sentences. Those are the ones you want to steer towards, and steering is
allowed.

## The honest version

If you built it with an agent, that is fine and increasingly normal. What is not
fine is not having read it.

The fix is not to hide it. The fix is to go through it now, line by line, and
make every decision in there <Circle>a decision you can defend</Circle>. That is
the same work as understanding it, which is what the viva is measuring anyway.
