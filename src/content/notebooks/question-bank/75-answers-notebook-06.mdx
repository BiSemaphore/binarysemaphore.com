**Q134** Where should latency be measured?  *<Ref to="/scaling">Notebook 06</Ref>, <Ref to="#2-framing-the-problem">section 2</Ref>.*

<Peach>At the client, not in the handler.</Peach> A handler that reports 40ms tells you
nothing about the 300ms the request spent queued in your load balancer, the
150ms of TLS handshake, or the 200ms of network to a user in another continent.
Server-side timings are for finding the bottleneck once you know there is one.
Client-side or synthetic timings are for knowing whether there is one. Mature
teams keep both and know they will not match.

**Q135** Can I trade latency for throughput?  *<Ref to="/scaling">Notebook 06</Ref>, <Ref to="#4-the-handoff">section 4</Ref>.*

Yes, deliberately, and it is often the right call. Batching is the standard
example: waiting 10ms to collect writes and flushing them in one round trip
raises every individual request's latency by up to 10ms and can multiply total
throughput several times over. The same trade sits behind connection pooling,
group commit and the Nagle algorithm. <Mint>State which one you are optimising for
before you tune anything</Mint>, because a change that helps one usually hurts the
other.

**Q136** We have 20 worker threads and P99 is climbing. Where do I start?  *<Ref to="/scaling">Notebook 06</Ref>, <Ref to="#5-the-queue">section 5</Ref>.*

Little's Law. At 500 rps and 200ms you need 100 concurrent slots to keep up, and
you have 20. <Peach>The threads are not slow, there are not enough of them</Peach>, and
every request beyond the twentieth is queueing before it starts. Either raise
concurrency, cut W, or shed load. Adding CPU will not help, because the CPU is
not what is scarce.

**Q139** Is tracing every request too expensive?  *<Ref to="/scaling">Notebook 06</Ref>, <Ref to="#9-the-interview-itself">section 9</Ref>.*

Yes, at volume, which is why nobody does it. You sample: typically 1% of normal
traffic plus 100% of anything that errored or exceeded a latency threshold, a
strategy called <Mint>tail-based sampling</Mint>. You keep the interesting traces and
throw away the boring ones. The overhead of the instrumentation itself is
usually a low single-digit percentage, which is cheap compared to a week spent
guessing.

**Q142** Is N+1 always bad?  *<Ref to="/scaling">Notebook 06</Ref>, <Ref to="#11-opening">section 11</Ref>.*

No, and saying so is a useful nuance. For N of three, on a warm connection, the
extra round trips cost microseconds and the batched version is harder to read.
<Mint>The problem is unbounded N.</Mint> If N is the size of a page and pages are capped
at 50, you have a small constant. If N is "however many line items this order
has", you have a bug waiting for your largest customer. Ask what bounds N; that
is the real question.

**Q143** Why did my index not get used?  *<Ref to="/scaling">Notebook 06</Ref>, <Ref to="#13-three-kinds-of-storage">section 13</Ref>.*

Four common reasons, and having them ready is a strong answer. <Mint>The column is
wrapped in a function</Mint> (`WHERE lower(email) = ?` will not use an index on
`email`; index the expression instead). <Mint>A leading column is missing</Mint>, per the
prefix rule above. <Mint>The planner thinks a scan is cheaper</Mint>, which is often
correct on a small table or a low-selectivity predicate. <Mint>Statistics are
stale</Mint>, so run `ANALYZE`. <Ref to="/postgres">Notebook 04</Ref> <Ref to="#29-delivery">section 29</Ref> works through each.

**Q146** What happens when Redis goes down?  *<Ref to="/scaling">Notebook 06</Ref>, <Ref to="#19-final-review">section 19</Ref>.*

The honest answer is what separates candidates. <Peach>A cache in front of a database
that cannot survive a cache outage is not a cache, it is a load-bearing
dependency you have not budgeted for.</Peach> If your steady-state hit rate is 95%,
losing the cache means twenty times the query volume arriving at once, which
will take the database with it. Plan for it: fail open with a circuit breaker,
shed load while it recovers, and know from a load test whether your database can
take the cold-start traffic. Many teams discover the answer is no during the
incident.

**Q150** What about sticky sessions?  *<Ref to="/scaling">Notebook 06</Ref>, <Ref to="#24-the-client-react-and-redux">section 24</Ref>.*

They work and they are a trap. <Pink>Session affinity</Pink> pins a user to one instance
so the local session keeps working, which lets you skip the refactor. The costs:
load stops being balanced (one instance gets all the heavy users), an instance
dying logs out everyone pinned to it, and <Peach>you cannot deploy without dropping
sessions</Peach>. Autoscaling makes all three worse, because instances come and go by
design. It is a legitimate short-term bridge and a bad destination, and saying
exactly that is the answer.

**Q154** What read-write ratio should I assume?  *<Ref to="/scaling">Notebook 06</Ref>, <Ref to="#27-kafka">section 27</Ref>.*

Do not assume; measure it, and say so. The usual claim is 90/10 or 80/20, and it
is true for content-heavy applications and false for plenty of others. An
analytics ingest pipeline can be 95% writes. An internal admin tool can be
50/50. <Peach>Read replicas only help in proportion to how read-heavy you actually
are</Peach>, and quoting a measured ratio for your own system is a much better answer
than quoting the industry average.

**Q157** The queue is backed up and jobs are two hours old. What now?  *<Ref to="/scaling">Notebook 06</Ref>, <Ref to="#35-making-it-fast">section 35</Ref>.*

Answer in the order you would act, because the order is the answer. <Peach>First,
stop the bleeding</Peach>: is the arrival rate above the drain rate, or did workers
die? Little's Law tells you which, since queue depth divided by drain rate is
your current wait. Then decide whether to scale workers, which only helps if the
downstream can take it, or shed work by dropping low-value job types. Then look
for the poison message that is failing and retrying forever, because one of
those can consume a whole worker pool. And afterwards, <Mint>alert on queue age
rather than queue depth</Mint>, since depth without a drain rate tells you nothing.

**Q163** The interviewer says "assume infinite budget". Does that change your answer?  *<Ref to="/scaling">Notebook 06</Ref>, <Ref to="#42-the-browser-is-a-boundary-too">section 42</Ref>.*

Barely, and saying so is the point. <Peach>Money buys bigger machines and more of
them; it does not buy consistency, it does not buy round trips back from the
speed of light, and it does not buy away the coordination cost of a distributed
system.</Peach> Infinite budget removes vertical scaling's ceiling and the cost
argument against replicas. It leaves every hard problem in this book exactly
where it was.
