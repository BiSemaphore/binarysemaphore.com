The lecture's walkthrough of DNS resolution is accurate and worth compressing to
one page. A name is resolved by asking a chain of servers, and <Peach>every link in
that chain caches, which is why the full walk is rare.</Peach>

```text
   1. OS cache               already know it? done
   2. browser cache          browsers keep their own
   3. recursive resolver     your ISP, or 8.8.8.8, or 1.1.1.1
        │                    caches heavily. most queries stop here
        ├─▶ root server      "I don't know example.com, ask .com"
        ├─▶ TLD server       "ask ns1.example.com"
        └─▶ authoritative    "93.184.216.34"
   4. answer flows back, cached at every level on the way
```

**The consequence that matters for your systems is failover.** Every record has a
TTL, and that TTL is how long the world may keep believing an address that is no
longer correct.

```text
   TTL 86400 (a day)     changing an address takes up to a day to
                         propagate. resolvers will not ask again
   TTL 60                changes propagate in about a minute, at the
                         cost of far more queries
```

<Mint>Before a planned migration, lower the TTL well in advance, then move, then
raise it again.</Mint> Lowering it on the day does not help, because resolvers are
still holding the old record with the old long TTL.

<Peach>And some resolvers ignore your TTL entirely</Peach>, clamping it to their own minimum
or holding records far longer than instructed. This is the clearest example in the
book of a cache you do not control: you can publish a number and you cannot
enforce it, so <Mint>DNS-based failover is never instant and must never be the only
failover mechanism.</Mint>

<Signal>

"DNS TTL is a lower bound on propagation, not a guarantee, because resolvers may
hold records longer. So we lower TTLs days before a migration and never rely on
DNS alone for failover."

</Signal>

<KeyIdea>

<Peach>Every DNS layer caches, so a record change reaches the world at the speed of the
TTL you set previously, not the one you set today.</Peach>

</KeyIdea>
