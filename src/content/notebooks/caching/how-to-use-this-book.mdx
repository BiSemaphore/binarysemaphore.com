Caching is taught as an optimisation and it is not one. An optimisation makes the
same thing faster. <Peach>A cache makes a different thing faster: an older thing.</Peach>

Every cache in this book, from the L1 line on your processor to the CDN edge node
in São Paulo, works the same way. It answers a question using data that was
correct at some point in the past, and it is faster precisely because it did not
go and check. The speed is not free and it is not paid for in money. It is paid
for in <Mint>correctness, in a currency of seconds</Mint>.

The lecture behind this book is *Caching, the secret behind it all*
(`youtube.com/watch?v=estH64OkwxU`, 64 minutes). Its examples are good and its
coverage of where caches live is the right map. It spends almost no time on the
half of the subject that is hard, which is what happens when the cached answer is
wrong and someone notices.

#### The one question

<Peach>How wrong are you willing to be, and for how long?</Peach>

Every decision in caching is an answer to it. A TTL is a numeric answer. An
eviction policy is an answer about what you are willing to lose. An invalidation
strategy is an attempt to put a bound on the answer. A hit ratio is how often you
got away with it.

| Part | The question, aimed at |
|---|---|
| I | Whether to cache at all, and what it costs |
| II | Which layer is wrong, and for how long |
| III | When you accept the staleness |
| IV | How you bound it |
| V | What happens when the bet goes wrong at scale |
| VI | Telling someone else's cache how wrong it may be |
| VII | The one process holding all of it |

If you can state the staleness window for every cache in your system, you
understand your system. <Peach>Most engineers cannot state it for any of them</Peach>, which
is why the second half of this book exists.

#### What is in the lecture and what is not

Parts I and II are the lecture, expanded, with the numbers filled in. Parts III
to VII are mostly outside it. The lecture covers two caching strategies; there
are five, and the two it omits are the ones that fail interestingly. It covers
eviction and never covers invalidation, which is the harder of the two by a wide
margin. It never mentions the thundering herd, cache stampede, penetration, or
`Cache-Control`, and it makes one claim about write-through freshness that is
not true in any system with more than one server.

There are three direct corrections, each in a trap block.

#### A suggested route

1. Do the arithmetic in <Ref to="#3-the-arithmetic-of-a-hit-ratio">section 3</Ref> with a pen. It is the argument for everything
   else, and it has a result most people find surprising.
2. Part II is a map. Read it once and refer back.
3. Parts IV and V are the book. They are where interviews go and where
   production incidents come from.

<KeyIdea>

A cache is a deliberate decision to serve data you have not checked. <Peach>The
engineering is not in making it fast, it is in bounding how wrong it can be and
knowing what happens when it disappears.</Peach>

</KeyIdea>
