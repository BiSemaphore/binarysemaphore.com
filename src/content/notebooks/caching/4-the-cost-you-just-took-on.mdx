The lecture presents caching as close to free: less latency, less load, happier
users. Here is the other side of the ledger, because an interview will ask for it
and a production system will present it whether or not you asked.

**A consistency problem you did not have before.** Two copies, no mechanism
keeping them equal. Every subsequent part of this book is about managing this.

**A new dependency.** Your application now needs Redis to be up. If you did not
plan for its absence, <Peach>you have converted a fast path into a hard requirement</Peach>,
and <Ref to="#29-when-the-cache-goes-down">section 29</Ref> is about what that costs.

**A new failure mode that is worse than being slow.** A slow system is visibly
slow. <Peach>A stale system is confidently wrong</Peach>, and the user has no signal at all.
A customer who changed their email address and still sees the old one has no
reason to think a cache exists.

**Operational surface.** Memory limits, eviction policy, keyspace growth,
connection pools, a failover story, and one more thing to page someone about at
3am.

**Debugging difficulty.** "It works on my machine" becomes "it works on the
instance that has the fresh copy". <Peach>A bug that reproduces only on some requests
is the expensive kind</Peach>, and an in-process cache across four instances produces
exactly that.

**A number to hold on to.** The cheapest cache is the one you did not add. Before
adding one, the questions in order are:

```text
   1. can the query be made fast enough?          an index, usually
   2. can the work be done once, in advance?      a materialised view,
                                                  a scheduled job
   3. can the client avoid asking?                HTTP caching, part VI
   4. now cache it
```

<Mint>Steps 1 and 2 have no consistency cost and are skipped constantly</Mint>, because
adding Redis is more interesting than reading a query plan. <Ref to="/postgres">Notebook 04</Ref> covers
the index question and <Ref to="/scaling">Notebook 06</Ref> covers when the answer is genuinely capacity.

<Trap>

<Rose>"We added Redis and the endpoint got faster, so it was the right call."</Rose>

It got faster and it also became wrong sometimes, in a way that will surface as a
support ticket rather than an alert. <Mint>The right call requires knowing what the
staleness window is and confirming that someone who understands the product finds
it acceptable.</Mint> "How out of date may this be?" is a product question, and
engineers answer it silently by picking a TTL that felt reasonable.

</Trap>

<KeyIdea>

<Peach>A cache trades correctness for speed, adds a dependency, and creates a failure
mode with no user-visible signal.</Peach> Try the index and the precomputation first,
because neither of them costs you any of that.

</KeyIdea>
