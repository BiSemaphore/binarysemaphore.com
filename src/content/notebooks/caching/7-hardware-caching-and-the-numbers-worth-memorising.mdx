The lecture covers L1, L2 and L3 briefly and correctly. The part worth carrying
into an interview is not the topology but the relative costs, because they let
you reason about anything.

| Operation | Time | Relative |
|---|---|---|
| L1 reference | 1 ns | 1 |
| L3 reference | 30 ns | 30 |
| Main memory reference | 100 ns | 100 |
| SSD random read | 100 µs | <Peach>100,000</Peach> |
| Round trip, same datacentre | 500 µs | 500,000 |
| Disk seek | 10 ms | 10,000,000 |
| Round trip, London to Sydney | 150 ms | <Peach>150,000,000</Peach> |

<Mint>Memorise three of them: memory is 100ns, an SSD read is 100µs, a cross-world
round trip is 150ms.</Mint> Everything else can be estimated from those, and being
able to say "that is three orders of magnitude" out loud is worth more than any
exact figure.

**The bottom row is the one that governs system design.** <Peach>No amount of
optimisation defeats the speed of light.</Peach> London to Sydney and back is roughly
150ms because the distance is 17,000 kilometres and light in fibre travels at
about two thirds of `c`. That number cannot be improved by better hardware, which
is why the only answer is to not make the trip, which is what a CDN is.

<Ask>

"Why is a CDN faster than a bigger origin server?"

Because the limit is distance, not capacity. <Peach>A request from Sydney to London
costs 150ms before the server has done anything at all</Peach>, and doubling the
server's CPU does not change that. The CDN's entire contribution is moving the
answer to within a few milliseconds of the user, which is the only lever that
works on the dominant term.

</Ask>

<KeyIdea>

<Peach>Memory is 100ns, SSD is 100µs, the far side of the world is 150ms.</Peach> The last
one is physics and is the reason edge caching exists.

</KeyIdea>
