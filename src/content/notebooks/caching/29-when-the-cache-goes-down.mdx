The question every interviewer asks and most systems have never tested.
<Peach>Redis is not there. What happens?</Peach>

**The failure most people have built without meaning to:**

```text
   1. Redis becomes unreachable
   2. every request now misses
   3. every request hits the database
   4. the database is sized for 10% of traffic         ← section 3
   5. the database falls over
   6. the site is down, not slow
```

You did not build a fast system with a cache. <Peach>You built a system that requires
a cache and does not say so.</Peach> The database was quietly resized around the hit
ratio, and the cache became a hard dependency the day someone provisioned for
steady-state load.

**Decide deliberately, and the answer is usually fail open:**

| Behaviour | When it is right |
|---|---|
| <Peach>Fail open</Peach> | <Mint>Almost always for reads.</Mint> Miss, hit the origin, be slow |
| Fail closed | The origin cannot survive the load. Shed instead |
| Serve stale | Best of both, if you have a second layer holding old values |

<Mint>Fail open needs three things to be safe</Mint>, and each is a line of configuration
people leave at its default:

```text
   1. a short connect and command timeout      50ms, not 5 seconds
   2. a circuit breaker                        stop trying after N failures,
                                               retry occasionally
   3. origin protection                        rate limit or shed, so a cold
                                               cache degrades rather than
                                               collapses         ← Notebook 06
```

<Peach>Without the timeout, a hung Redis is worse than a missing one</Peach>: every request
waits five seconds for a connection that will never open, your threads or
connections exhaust, and the outage is total rather than partial. This is the most
common way a cache outage becomes a full outage.

**The in-process cache is your friend here.** A small local cache with a few
seconds of TTL keeps the hottest keys answerable even when Redis is gone. It costs
you the consistency described in <Ref to="#12-the-application-cache-redis-and-memcached">section 12</Ref>, where N instances hold N answers, and
<Mint>for a few seconds of staleness on hot data, that is usually a trade worth
making.</Mint>

<DoThis>

Test it. <Mint>Kill Redis in staging under realistic load and watch what happens.</Mint>
This is a fifteen-minute experiment that reliably finds a missing timeout, and
almost nobody runs it before the incident rather than after.

</DoThis>

<KeyIdea>

<Peach>If your database cannot serve 100% of traffic, your cache is a hard
dependency.</Peach> Set aggressive timeouts, fail open, protect the origin, and test the
outage before it happens.

</KeyIdea>
