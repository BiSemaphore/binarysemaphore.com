The lecture's definition is good and worth keeping: <Peach>a subset of some data, held
somewhere faster to reach.</Peach> Three words in that sentence carry the weight.

<Term name="Cache">

A store holding a subset of data from a slower, larger, authoritative source, so
that repeated requests can be answered without consulting it. <Peach>The authoritative
source is still authoritative</Peach>; the cache is a copy that is allowed to be wrong.

</Term>

**Subset**, because if it held everything it would be a replica and would need
the capacity and the durability of the original. The lecture makes this point
well with Netflix: no edge node holds the catalogue, and choosing what each one
holds is itself a machine learning problem.

**Faster**, which is always a trade against something. Every layer of the
hierarchy in <Ref to="#7-hardware-caching-and-the-numbers-worth-memorising">section 7</Ref> buys speed with capacity, cost, or durability, and usually
all three.

**A copy**, and this is the word the lecture does not press on. <Peach>The moment the
copy exists, there are two answers to the same question and no mechanism keeping
them equal.</Peach> Everything difficult about caching descends from that sentence, and
it is why Parts IV and V are longer than Parts II and III.

**The vocabulary, once, so the rest of the book can use it:**

```text
   cache hit        the data was there. you paid the cache lookup only
   cache miss       it was not. you paid the lookup AND the full path
   hit ratio        hits / (hits + misses). the number that matters
   eviction         removing something because you are out of room
   invalidation     removing something because it is WRONG
   TTL              time to live: how long a copy may be used
   stale            still present, no longer correct
```

<Mint>Eviction and invalidation are different problems and people conflate them
constantly.</Mint> Eviction is a capacity question: what do I drop to make room?
Invalidation is a correctness question: what is no longer true? <Peach>The lecture
covers eviction thoroughly and invalidation not at all</Peach>, which is the single
largest gap in it, and Part IV is the answer.

<KeyIdea>

<Peach>A cache is a copy that is allowed to be wrong.</Peach> Eviction is about running out
of space; invalidation is about running out of truth. They are solved by
different mechanisms and confusing them is the most common conceptual error in
this subject.

</KeyIdea>
