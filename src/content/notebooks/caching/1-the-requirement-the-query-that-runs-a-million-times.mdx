partTitle: "Caching is a bet about staleness"
---
You have a dashboard. It shows each organisation its project count, its most
active members, and a chart of tasks completed per week. The query behind it
joins four tables, aggregates over a few million rows, and takes 400ms.

That is acceptable. One user, once, is fine.

Then the product succeeds. Ten thousand users open the dashboard each morning
within the same twenty minutes, and the arithmetic changes:

```text
   10,000 users  ×  400ms of database work
   ────────────────────────────────────────
   =  4,000 seconds of query time, in a 20 minute window

   20 minutes = 1,200 seconds of wall clock
   so you need  4,000 / 1,200  ≈  3.3 cores of pure database CPU
   doing nothing but this one query
```

<Peach>And almost every one of those ten thousand answers is identical.</Peach> The
underlying tasks changed a handful of times during the window. You computed the
same aggregate ten thousand times because nobody told the system it had already
done it.

This is the shape the lecture identifies correctly across all three of its
examples. Google recomputing rankings for the same query, Netflix streaming the
same film from Oregon to Mumbai, Twitter recalculating trends for every viewer
of the trending page. <Mint>Expensive work, repeated, with the same answer.</Mint>

**But notice what makes it cacheable, because it is not "it is expensive".** It
is that <Peach>the answer changes far more slowly than it is requested.</Peach> Trending
topics shift over hours and are read every second. A film does not change at all.
A dashboard aggregate moves a few times an hour and is read ten thousand times.

That ratio is the whole decision:

```text
   read rate                 how often the answer is requested
   ─────────────      the higher this is, the more a cache buys
   change rate               how often the answer is different
```

A value read a thousand times a second and written a thousand times a second is
<Peach>a terrible caching candidate</Peach> no matter how expensive it is to compute,
because every cached copy is wrong almost immediately.

<Signal>

"I do not decide what to cache by looking at what is slow. I look at the ratio of
read rate to change rate, because that ratio is what determines whether a cache
will be right often enough to be worth its consistency cost."

</Signal>

<KeyIdea>

<Peach>Cache what is read far more often than it changes.</Peach> Expense is what makes a
cache worth building; the read-to-write ratio is what makes it correct often
enough to be worth having.

</KeyIdea>
