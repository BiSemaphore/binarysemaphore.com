title: "POST, and why it is the odd one"

partTitle: "The method is a promise about repeating"
---
`POST` creates, and it is the only method that promises nothing. Neither safe nor
idempotent: two identical `POST` requests are two distinct requests, and the
server is expected to act on both.

```text
   POST /organizations   {"name": "Acme"}   ──▶  201, id 8f2a
   POST /organizations   {"name": "Acme"}   ──▶  201, id 3d10

   the same bytes, twice, and now you have two organisations
```

The lecture makes exactly the right observation about why this happens: the
identifier is assigned by the server, usually by the database, so <Peach>the second
request cannot be recognised as a repeat of the first because it does not carry
anything that identifies the first.</Peach> There is nothing in the payload to collide
on unless you have imposed a uniqueness constraint yourself.

**And `POST` is deliberately open-ended.** The specification defines it as
requesting that the target resource process the enclosed representation according
to its own semantics, which is close to saying "whatever this endpoint means".
That vagueness is a feature: it is the escape hatch for every operation that is
not a create, read, update or delete, and Part VI is about using it well.

<Mint>The consequence to hold on to is that `POST` is the method you choose when you
cannot make a promise.</Mint> If an operation genuinely is repeatable without harm,
`PUT` says so and buys you retries for free. Reach for `POST` when it is not, and
then read <Ref to="#20-making-post-retryable-idempotency-keys">section 20</Ref>, because "not idempotent" is a problem to solve rather than
a label to apply.

<Ask>

"If `POST` is not idempotent, how does anything survive a mobile network?"

It does not, by default, and this is the practical reason the question matters.
A client that sends a `POST`, loses the connection, and never sees the response
has no way to know whether the order was placed. Its choices are to retry and
risk duplicating, or not retry and risk losing. <Mint><Ref to="#20-making-post-retryable-idempotency-keys">Section 20</Ref>'s idempotency keys
exist precisely to give the client a third option</Mint>, and "we would add an
idempotency key" is the answer this question is fishing for.

</Ask>

<KeyIdea>

<Peach>`POST` means "I cannot promise anything about repeating this".</Peach> That is why it
creates, why it absorbs every custom action, and why it is the only method that
needs extra machinery to be safely retried.

</KeyIdea>
