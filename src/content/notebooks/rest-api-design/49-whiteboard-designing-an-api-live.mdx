"Design the API for a project management tool." Forty minutes. The failure mode is
to start writing endpoints immediately; the endpoints are the easy part and doing
them first means doing them without information.

Work in this order, out loud:

```text
   1. NOUNS               "what are the resources? organisations, projects,
        │                  tasks, users, tags"                              6
        ▼                  two minutes, and it frames everything after
   2. ONE FULL CRUD       list, create, get, update, delete for ONE resource
        │                  establishes plural, nesting, codes, envelope     8, 21
        ▼                  then say: "the others follow this pattern"
   3. THE LIST ENDPOINT   pagination, sorting, filtering, and the caps
        │                  ← where they will probe. spend time here      30 to 38
        ▼
   4. THE CUSTOM ACTION   archive, or clone. name it, justify it            39
        │                  admit it is RPC before they ask                  41
        ▼
   5. THE FAILURES        401 vs 403, 404 vs empty list, 409, the
        │                  error shape                                  23 to 29
        ▼
   6. THE HARD PARTS      retries and idempotency keys, concurrent
                          edits and ETags, versioning policy         20, 27, 12
```

**Step 2 is the one to be disciplined about.** <Peach>Design one resource completely
rather than five resources partially.</Peach> A complete `organizations` family
demonstrates every convention you have, and "projects and tasks follow the same
pattern" is a sentence an interviewer will accept immediately, because in a real
codebase it is also the correct answer.

**Step 3 is where the interview actually happens.** Anyone can produce five CRUD
routes. The list endpoint is where pagination correctness, index awareness, cost
control and defaults all live, and it is where a senior answer separates itself.
<Mint>Volunteer the page-2 bug before you are asked.</Mint>

**Step 6 is what most candidates never reach**, usually because they spent twenty
minutes on steps 1 and 2. <Peach>Getting to retries, concurrency and versioning is
worth more than a complete endpoint list</Peach>, so move briskly through the parts you
know are routine and say that you are doing so.

<DoThis>

Say the trade-off out loud every time you make one. "I am using keyset here, which
means no jump-to-page-7; if the product needs numbered pages I would use offset
with a depth cap." <Mint>A named trade is the difference between someone recalling a
rule and someone designing.</Mint>

</DoThis>

<KeyIdea>

<Peach>Nouns, one full CRUD, the list endpoint, the action, the failures, the hard
parts.</Peach> Depth on one resource beats breadth across five, and the last step is
worth the most.

</KeyIdea>
